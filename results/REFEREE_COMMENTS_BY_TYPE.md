# The referee comments, read from the reports, split by model vs empirical

Read directly from `Referee report.pdf` (R1, 5 pp.) and `Report (1).pdf`
(R2, 4 pp.). Paraphrased, not quoted.

**How they count.** R1 has 5 major and 4 minor numbered items; R2 has 6 major
and 2 minor. That is 17 numbers, but R1's Major 1 has three lettered parts,
R1's Major 2 asks two separable things and R2's Major 5 asks two, which makes
**21 distinct requests**.

## MODEL — 7

| # | What it asks |
|---|---|
| R1 M2 | Separate the pure dispersion effect from the volatility-induced rise in the expected oil price, using the mean-preserving specification written out in the report, and recompute the responses of default probabilities, spreads and borrowing. |
| R1 M2 (second part) | Clarify the precautionary-borrowing mechanism of Section 6.3: once the mean effect is removed, does any remaining increase in borrowing reflect a response to oil price risk itself rather than to a higher expected price level? |
| R1 M5 | The precautionary-borrowing result assumes risk-neutral lenders. Discuss how robust it is to lender-side risk pricing, state that its strength is conditional on risk-neutral pricing, and if feasible add a robustness exercise with an exogenous SDF or a reduced-form global risk premium. |
| R2 M1 | Part of what the paper calls an uncertainty effect is a first-moment effect, since E[p'] = exp(m + v/2). Subtract ½Var(log p' \| σ) from the conditional mean so volatility states differ only in dispersion, then reinterpret. A small pure-uncertainty effect is an acceptable answer. |
| R2 M2 | The welfare and spread comparisons mix less risk with cheaper oil: the basic model fixes the price at the median of the log-normal, not its mean, and the full and intermediate economies also differ in average price. Construct the comparison economies to match the full model's average price level. |
| R2 M3 | A country in default keeps importing oil frictionlessly. Discuss this assumption; better, add a robustness exercise where default also disrupts oil imports, through a price wedge or partial exclusion during autarky. |
| R1 m2 (model part) | A robustness exercise in which default costs depend on total output or total resources, rather than on the exogenous endowment. |

## EMPIRICAL — 6

| # | What it asks |
|---|---|
| R1 M1a | Direct evidence that higher oil price volatility raises expectations of future oil prices — for example from oil futures or survey forecasts such as Consensus Forecasts. |
| R1 M1b | Empirical analogues of the state dependence in Figures 3 and 5: interact oil price changes with GDP growth, output gaps or recession indicators, and with debt-to-GDP. |
| R1 M1c | Table 1 clusters only by country, while oil price changes and volatility are common across countries within a quarter. Report two-way clustered standard errors, by country and by quarter. |
| R1 m3 | Interact oil price shocks or volatility with exchange-rate depreciation, or where available with foreign-currency debt exposure. |
| R2 M5a | Test the headline state dependence directly: interact the change in the oil price with a high-volatility indicator, or with volatility itself, in equation (1). |
| R2 M5b | Test whether spreads of more oil-dependent economies respond more strongly to oil price volatility, by interacting volatility with the country-level oil import share already computed from WITS. |

## BOTH — 4

| # | What it asks |
|---|---|
| R1 M3 | Discipline the CES substitution elasticity with external evidence on energy demand or imported-input substitution **(empirical)**; show how oil imports, the value of oil imports, output, default probabilities and spreads respond over a plausible range **(model)**; and discuss the implications of imposing one elasticity on 35 heterogeneous economies. |
| R1 M4 | Sharpen what the oil-specific structure adds relative to Seoane (2019) and Johri–Khan–Sosa-Padilla (2022), since the same structure would apply to any important imported input **(exposition)**; test whether the effect is stronger where oil import dependence is greater and control for broader imported-input or producer-price volatility **(empirical)**; and, if feasible, compare against a generic imported-input price process matched on persistence and unconditional volatility **(model)**. |
| R2 M6 | The model's effect is far smaller than the data's: slopes of 0.05 and 0.15 imply 0.5 and 1.5 bp per 10% oil move against a mean spread of 410 bp, while the Table 1 coefficients of 0.14–0.19 imply roughly 6–8 bp. Discuss what governs the magnitude **(model)**, and estimate regression (1) on model-simulated data, with volatility in the same units, so the coefficients are comparable **(both)**. |
| R2 m2 | Table 3 contains no oil-related moment: no correlation of the simulated spread with the oil price or its volatility, no volatility of the oil import share, and no comparison of the model's default frequency with the data. |

## EXPOSITION ONLY — 4

| # | What it asks |
|---|---|
| R2 M4 | The novelty claim is too strong as written. The paper says "none of the existing research has addressed the impact of oil-related volatility shocks within the framework of sovereign default literature", but its own reference list has Lopez-Martin et al. (2019), a default model with stochastic commodity revenues and hedging, cited but not discussed, and its own footnote 1 acknowledges oil-and-default work for exporters (Hamann et al. 2018; Esquivel 2020). Restate the gap as oil importers and estimated stochastic volatility in oil prices specifically, and differentiate from the closest papers in detail. |
| R1 m1 | Stop using "oil price uncertainty" and "oil price volatility" interchangeably; the analysis is about time-varying conditional volatility, while uncertainty also covers disagreement, forecast dispersion and ambiguity. Use "volatility" throughout unless a broader claim is intended. |
| R1 m4 | Typo on page 12 and in Table 2: for the stated CES specification the elasticity of substitution is 1/(1−θ), not 1/θ. At θ = 0.5 both equal 2, so the benchmark value is unaffected. |
| R2 m1 | The bond price is written q(b′,y) in equation (4) and below (8), but the schedule in (11) and in the timing section depends on (b′,y,p,σ). Also, the CES elasticity in equation (3) is 1/(1−ν), not 1/ν. |

(R1 m2 also asks, expositionally, that the exogenous endowment be distinguished
from endogenous final output throughout; its model half is listed above.)
